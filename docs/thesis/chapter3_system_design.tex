\chapter{Overall System Design}
\label{chap:system_design}

\section{Chapter Overview}
\label{sec:design_overview}

This chapter presents the system-level design of the BeatWeaver. The
system receives a causal audio stream and produces a continuous sequence of
poses for a 29-degree-of-freedom Unitree G1 model. It does not synthesise a new
dance from scratch. Instead, it retrieves music-related motions from a
prevalidated catalogue, decides when a change is justified, adapts motion
timing to musical events, and connects successive trajectories without an
abrupt change in pose or root motion.

The central architectural decision is to separate an offline preparation path
from an online decision-and-playback path. Computationally expensive or
non-causal operations, including human-to-robot retargeting, whole-clip motion
analysis, catalogue normalisation, and trajectory preflight, are performed
offline. The online path is limited to rolling-window audio analysis,
catalogue search, stateful selection, candidate preparation, beat--phase
control, transition planning, and constrained pose output. A motion can enter
the live candidate set only after its source, representation, and target-robot
validity have been established.

The BeatWeaver is therefore a hybrid system rather than an end-to-end
neural network. A pretrained music model supplies learned audio
representations, explicit DSP features describe rhythm and activity,
deterministic scoring ranks catalogue items, and state machines govern
selection and switching. Retargeted G1 trajectories supply the motion content,
while a constrained controller governs timing and continuity. Each component
has a narrow responsibility and exposes diagnostic values that can be tested
independently. Chapter~\ref{chap:offline_catalogue} specifies the offline data
construction in detail, and Chapter~\ref{chap:realtime_matching_control}
defines the online algorithms and their parameters.



\section{End-to-End Architecture}
\label{sec:design_architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:humanoid_matcher_architecture} summarises the architecture. The
offline path supplies a frozen catalogue. The online path begins with a rolling
audio buffer, proceeds through retrieval and selection, and ends with a robot
pose after phase control, transition blending, and output checks.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter3_end_to_end_architecture.pdf}
    \caption{End-to-end architecture of the BeatWeaver}
    \label{fig:humanoid_matcher_architecture}
\end{figure}

The main information flow is

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal{A}_{\leq t}
    &\longrightarrow \mathbf{x}_t
    \longrightarrow \mathcal{T}_t
    \longrightarrow \mathcal{C}_t
    \longrightarrow d_t \\
    &\longrightarrow (i_t,\phi_t,T_t)
    \longrightarrow \mathbf{q}^{\mathrm{target}}_t
    \longrightarrow \mathbf{q}^{\mathrm{out}}_t,
\end{split}
\label{eq:overall_information_flow}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the descriptor of the recent audio,
$\mathcal{T}_t$ is the ranked track set, $\mathcal{C}_t$ is the ranked motion
set, and $d_t$ is the stateful selection decision. A committed switch specifies
a target motion $i_t$, an entry phase $\phi_t$, and a transition duration $T_t$.
$\mathbf{q}^{\mathrm{target}}_t$ is the music-timed and, where necessary,
blended target pose. $\mathbf{q}^{\mathrm{out}}_t$ is the pose after final
output conditioning.

The notation makes two boundaries explicit. A ranked motion is not yet a switch
decision, and a switch decision is not yet a safe output pose. These boundaries
prevent ranking jitter from changing the motion immediately, prevent an
unloaded candidate from stalling the switch boundary, and prevent a relevant
candidate from bypassing continuity and output checks.

The online architecture uses bounded background workers. Audio feature
extraction and retrieval run outside the foreground playback path. Candidate
motions are loaded, grounded, and prepared before the next eligible commitment
point. The foreground path consumes only completed results and never waits for
retargeting, feature extraction, or file loading. Coalescing retrieval work and
bounding candidate caches prevents stale evidence or speculative preparation
from accumulating without limit.

\section{Subsystem Boundaries and Contracts}
\label{sec:design_subsystem_boundaries}

\subsection{Offline preparation contract}
\label{subsec:design_offline_contract}

The offline subsystem converts paired AIST++ music and SMPL motion
\cite{li2021ai,loper2023smpl} into three linked catalogue units: audio segments,
music tracks, and motion profiles. Segments provide local evidence for a live
query, tracks aggregate related segments without rewarding duplicated or long
recordings, and motion profiles distinguish choreographies associated with the
same music.

Catalogue and query audio must use the same extractor identity, model revision,
sample rate, window policy, and feature dimensions. Learned embedding and
rhythm--timbre vectors are compared using statistics stored from the offline
reference set. Style activations retain their label-coordinate meaning and are
unit-normalised rather than z-standardised. This is a versioned interface, not
an informal cache whose meaning depends on whichever code happens to load it.

On the motion side, the offline path detects a small set of salient entry
hypotheses, computes activity and regularity summaries, and retargets the human
sequence into a canonical named-joint G1 trajectory. Provenance and kinematic
preflight determine admission. Only a joined record whose audio, source motion,
profile, retargeted artefact, and preflight result are all present is exposed to
the online matcher. The feature definitions, retargeting procedure, validation
thresholds, and current catalogue statistics are given once in
Chapter~\ref{chap:offline_catalogue}.

\subsection{Online perception and selection contract}
\label{subsec:design_online_contract}

The online subsystem processes the same causal stream at two timescales. A
longer rolling window supplies relatively stable evidence for music and motion
retrieval, while short audio blocks supply activity, onset, and beat events for
timing.

Retrieval is hierarchical. Segment comparison first supplies local musical
evidence, which is pooled at track level and may be restricted by style and
abstention rules. A second stage evaluates whether linked motions are compatible
with the observed tempo, event density, and activity. The component scores are
retained because music similarity and motion feasibility have different
failure modes. Their equations, weights, thresholds, and missing-evidence rules
are defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:realtime_matching_control} rather than repeated
here.

Selection adds temporal state to these rankings. Sustained evidence can create
a pending relevance selection, whereas a separate bounded-hold policy may
request diversity among similarly relevant motions. Both policies are subject
to the same readiness and commitment rules. Rejected or weak audio clears new
relevance accumulation and holds the active motion instead of forcing an
arbitrary catalogue item.

\subsection{Prepared switching and execution contract}
\label{subsec:design_execution_contract}

A pending identifier expresses intent but does not authorise immediate
playback. Preparation loads and validates the canonical trajectory, grounds it
in the playback model, adapts its named joints to the active model, and builds
the state features required for entry evaluation. Accepted beats define the
musical commitment points shared by selection and phase control. If the
preferred item is not prepared, the system may use only another ranked and
prepared candidate. Otherwise it continues or safely completes the current
trajectory.

The logical progression is

\begin{equation}
    \texttt{current}
    \rightarrow \texttt{evidence}
    \rightarrow \texttt{pending}
    \rightarrow \texttt{prepared}
    \rightarrow \texttt{transitioning}
    \rightarrow \texttt{current}.
\label{eq:switch_state_sequence}
\end{equation}

At a valid commitment point, entry search compares the current state with a
small neighbourhood around salient target poses. Pose, velocity, support,
root-state, and musical-salience disagreement determine entry quality. The
transition duration then reflects the required joint displacement, configured
dynamics limits, remaining target duration, and musical timing. These
calculations are online concerns and are specified in
Chapter~\ref{chap:realtime_matching_control}.

During playback, accepted beats modify motion speed and consume phase error
gradually. They do not teleport the sampler to a different pose. Target root
motion is aligned to the current world trajectory before blending. One-shot
clips do not cross an implicit last-to-first seam, and completion transfers the
sampler, phase controller, root references, and motion identifier atomically.
The resulting pose passes through stateful joint limits. Runtime collision
handling, when enabled, is applied to the pose that would otherwise be emitted
and must update the limiter state if it changes that pose. Offline preflight
does not by itself imply that this optional online collision policy is active.

\section{State Ownership and Failure Isolation}
\label{sec:design_state_ownership}

The system assigns each form of state to one responsible component. The
retriever owns the latest completed ranking and its rejection status. The
selector owns evidence streaks, pending reason, held-bar state, and recency
history. The preparation pool owns candidate readiness and load failures. Each
motion controller owns phase, accepted-beat history, amplitude state, and rate
limits. The transition controller owns source--target blending and transfers
the new active state only at completion. Finally, the output layer owns the
previous emitted joint state used by its dynamics limiter.

Figure~\ref{fig:state_ownership_swimlanes} illustrates a representative
asynchronous switch. The foreground columns distinguish logical state owners
within the same playback loop, rather than independent execution threads.
Retrieval and preparation return completed results while active-motion sampling
and output conditioning continue. Candidate readiness and an eligible musical
boundary permit commitment; the active playback state changes at blend
completion. Background entry scoring uses a generation check to discard plans
invalidated by newer retrieval evidence.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter3_state_ownership_swimlanes.pdf}
    \caption{State ownership and asynchronous execution during a representative
    prepared switch}
    \label{fig:state_ownership_swimlanes}
\end{figure}

This separation prevents a partial failure from corrupting the active motion.
A busy retrieval worker skips a submission opportunity rather than building a
backlog. A loading or grounding failure removes the affected candidate from the
ready set. A rejected query leaves the current motion active. If a one-shot
clip ends before another evidence-backed candidate is prepared, its terminal
pose is held instead of wrapping through an unauthorised seam or blocking on
file I/O.

Diagnostics follow the same ownership boundaries. Retrieval exposes rankings
and rejection reasons. Selection exposes evidence, pending reason, and
readiness. Timing exposes accepted beats and remaining phase error. Transition
planning exposes entry quality and blend progress, and output conditioning
exposes limiter and collision interventions. Chapter~\ref{chap:realtime_matching_control}
defines the concrete trace fields used by later experiments.

The present output is a kinematic trajectory preview and geometric validation
path in MuJoCo \cite{todorov2012mujoco}, not a torque-controlled balance
simulation. Canonical retargeting, kinematic preflight, and optional online
collision handling improve representation and geometric validity, but they do
not prove dynamic trackability or hardware safety. Those claims require a
separate controller and experimental evaluation.
